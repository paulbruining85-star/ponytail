# Question bank — reconstructing the work situation

Where to look, not boxes to fill. The areas follow the broad cause categories
used in public root-cause and human-factors guidance (procedures, equipment and
design, work environment, training, supervision and management, communication —
see `sources.md`). For each question, note the answer *and* how it was obtained.

## Task and instruction
- Is there a written instruction for this exact task? Which version was in use that day?
- Could the person reach it at the point of work, and follow it without guessing?
- Is it correct? Walk through it step by step with someone who does the task.
- Do experienced people actually follow it as written? If not, what do they do instead, and why?
- Did anything about the task, the product, or the rule change recently?

## Equipment, labels, layout, interface
- Could the wrong action be done as easily as the right one (look-alike items, adjacent buttons, similar names)?
- Does anything stop or warn the person before the error becomes a consequence?
- Is the needed information visible where the decision is made?

## Conditions at the time
- Workload and staffing that shift; any rush or deadline.
- Interruptions or distractions during the task.
- Time of day, shift length, overtime, noise, temperature, lighting.

## Knowledge for this task
- Was the person taught this specific rule or change? When, how, by whom? Is there a record?
- Was their understanding checked (demonstration, sign-off), or only attendance recorded?
- How often do they do this task? Routine, occasional, or first time?

## Supervision and management
- Who checks this work, and when? Did the check happen?
- Are there targets or pressures that pull against doing it the careful way?
- Were earlier near misses or complaints about this step raised? What happened to them?

## Handover and communication
- What information did the person need from someone else, and did it arrive complete and on time?
- Shift handover, verbal instructions, notes, system messages — where could it have been lost or misread?

## History and comparison (the substitution question)
- Has anyone else made the same or a similar mistake? Near misses count.
- How do two or three colleagues with similar experience do this task? Would they have been likely to do the same in the same situation?
- Is the "wrong" way actually the common way?

## Talking to the person involved
- Ask them to walk through what they were doing and what they were thinking at each point — what made sense at the time.
- Ask what would have helped. Ask what they think others would do.
- Make clear the purpose is to prevent it happening again, and keep that promise in the output.
